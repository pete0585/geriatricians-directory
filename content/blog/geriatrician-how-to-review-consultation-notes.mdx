## Why reviewing consultation notes matters

After a visit with a geriatrician, you or your family member may leave with a lot of information delivered in a short amount of time. Consultation notes are the written record of that visit. Reviewing them carefully at home can help you:

- Confirm what was discussed and what was recommended.
- Identify anything that was unclear or that you want to revisit.
- Prepare focused questions for a follow-up conversation.
- Keep a clear record you can share with other involved professionals, when appropriate.

This article is a practical guide for reviewing those notes. It is not medical advice, and it does not replace a conversation with a qualified professional. If anything in the notes concerns you, contact the geriatrician's office or another qualified professional.

## What consultation notes typically contain

Notes vary by practice and by the professional writing them. Common elements can include:

- **Reason for the visit** — the concern or question that prompted the appointment.
- **History** — background information gathered during the visit.
- **Observations** — what the geriatrician noted during the consultation.
- **Assessment or impression** — the geriatrician's working understanding of the situation.
- **Plan** — recommended next steps, referrals, follow-up timing, or areas to monitor.
- **Medications and orders** — changes, refills, or instructions, when applicable.

Because formats differ, your first task is simply to identify which sections exist in your notes and where each piece of information lives.

## A checklist for reviewing your notes

Use the following checklist as a structured way to work through the document. You do not need to complete every item in one sitting.

### 1. Confirm the basics

- [ ] Is the patient name and date of visit correct?
- [ ] Is the geriatrician's name and practice information accurate?
- [ ] Are the contact details for follow-up included?

If any of these are wrong, contact the office to have them corrected.

### 2. Separate facts from impressions

- [ ] Which statements describe what was reported or observed?
- [ ] Which statements reflect the geriatrician's assessment or interpretation?

Distinguishing these two helps you understand where there may be room for discussion. An assessment is a professional's working view, not a fixed verdict.

### 3. Identify the plan

- [ ] What follow-up timing, if any, was recommended?
- [ ] Were any referrals suggested?
- [ ] Were any monitoring or lifestyle suggestions noted?
- [ ] Were any medication instructions recorded?

Write these out in your own words. If you cannot restate a recommendation clearly, that is a good candidate for a follow-up question.

### 4. Flag anything unclear

- [ ] Are there abbreviations or terms you do not recognize?
- [ ] Are there statements that seem to conflict with each other?
- [ ] Is anything missing that you expected to see?

Keep a running list. You do not need to resolve these on your own.

### 5. Note your own questions

- [ ] What would you like clarified?
- [ ] What would you like to revisit at the next visit?
- [ ] Is there information you want to add that was not captured?

### 6. Decide on next steps

- [ ] Do you need to schedule a follow-up?
- [ ] Do you need to request a copy or correction of the notes?
- [ ] Do you want to share the notes with another professional involved in care?

## Hypothetical examples (illustrative only)

The following examples are invented for illustration. They are not drawn from real patients, real providers, or real cases, and they do not describe clinical outcomes.

**Example A — The unclear abbreviation.**

*Scenario:* A reader reviews notes and sees an abbreviation they do not recognize in the plan section. They are unsure whether it refers to a follow-up interval or a type of referral.

*Possible approach:* Write the abbreviation down, note the sentence it appeared in, and ask the geriatrician's office what it means during a follow-up call. Avoid guessing based on internet searches alone, since abbreviations can have multiple meanings.

**Example B — The missing detail.**

*Scenario:* A reader remembers discussing a specific concern during the visit, but it does not appear anywhere in the notes.

*Possible approach:* Note the concern and the approximate time in the visit when it came up, then raise it at the next contact. It may simply not have been recorded, or it may have been addressed under a different heading.

**Example C — The conflicting statements.**

*Scenario:* One part of the notes suggests a follow-up in a few weeks, while another part seems to suggest a longer interval.

*Possible approach:* Highlight both statements and ask the office to clarify which applies. Do not assume one is correct and the other is an error without confirming.

**Example D — The unfamiliar term.**

*Scenario:* A reader encounters a term in the assessment section that they have not seen before.

*Possible approach:* Write the term down and ask for a plain-language explanation. Requesting clarification is a normal part of understanding your own or a family member's care.

**Example E — Preparing for a follow-up.**

*Scenario:* A reader has several questions after reviewing the notes and wants to make the most of a short follow-up call.

*Possible approach:* Group questions by theme (for example, follow-up timing, referrals, and daily routines), put the most important question first, and have the notes open during the call.

## Turning your review into useful questions

A careful review often produces a short list of questions. Useful questions tend to be specific and tied to something in the notes. For example:

- "In the plan section, it says to follow up in a certain timeframe. Can you confirm what that timeframe is?"
- "I see a referral mentioned. Can you tell me what that referral is for and who I should expect to hear from?"
- "There is a term in the assessment I do not recognize. Can you explain it in plain language?"
- "I mentioned a concern during the visit that I do not see in the notes. Can we revisit it?"

## When to contact a professional

Some situations call for prompt contact rather than waiting:

- If you notice something in the notes that suggests an urgent concern.
- If a medication instruction is unclear or appears inconsistent with what you were told.
- If you are unsure whether a recommended next step applies to you or your family member.

In these cases, contact the geriatrician's office or another qualified professional directly. Do not rely on notes alone to make decisions about care.

## Keeping notes organized over time

If you are coordinating care across multiple visits or providers, a simple system can help:

- Keep a folder or digital file for consultation notes.
- Add a short summary in your own words after each review.
- Track open questions in one place so nothing gets lost.
- Note the date you reviewed each set of notes.

This makes it easier to spot patterns, prepare for future visits, and share information when appropriate.

## A short recap

Reviewing geriatrician consultation notes is a practical skill. Confirm the basics, separate facts from impressions, identify the plan, flag anything unclear, and turn your review into a focused set of questions. Use the checklist above as a starting point, and adapt it to your situation.

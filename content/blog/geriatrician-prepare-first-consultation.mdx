# Geriatrician: How to Prepare for a First Consultation

A first consultation with a geriatrician can feel like a lot is riding on one appointment. You may be coordinating schedules, arranging transportation, and trying to summarize years of health history in a short window. The good news is that preparation is something you can control, and it tends to make the visit more useful for everyone involved.

This guide is a practical decision aid. It focuses on how to prepare, what to bring, what to ask, and how to follow up. It is not medical advice, and it does not replace a conversation with a qualified professional. If you have urgent symptoms or concerns, contact a qualified healthcare professional promptly.

## What a geriatrician focuses on

Geriatricians are physicians who focus on the health and well-being of older adults. Their work often involves complex, overlapping concerns: multiple medications, changes in mobility or memory, falls, sensory changes, and the social or practical side of daily life. A first visit is usually about building a full picture rather than solving everything at once.

Because of that, the most useful preparation is not a perfect summary. It is a clear, organized set of information that helps the clinician understand your situation and priorities.

## Start with your goals for the visit

Before you gather documents, decide what you most want from the consultation. Write down two or three goals. Examples:

- **Example (clearly hypothetical):** You want to understand whether a recent change in walking ability is something to monitor or something to address more actively.
- **Example (clearly hypothetical):** You want a second opinion on a medication list that has grown over several years.
- **Example (clearly hypothetical):** You want help coordinating care between a primary care clinician and a specialist.

Goals help the clinician focus the conversation. They also give you a way to measure whether the visit was useful.

## Build a one-page health summary

A one-page summary is often more helpful than a stack of papers. Aim for clarity, not completeness. Include:

- **Current concerns:** What has changed recently? When did it start?
- **Medical history:** Major conditions, surgeries, and hospitalizations, with approximate dates if you know them.
- **Medications:** A current list, including prescriptions, over-the-counter products, vitamins, and supplements. Include doses if available.
- **Allergies and reactions:** What happened, and when.
- **Providers:** Names and roles of the clinicians currently involved in care.
- **Support system:** Who helps with daily tasks, transportation, or decision-making.

If you are unsure about a detail, write "unsure" rather than guessing. Accuracy matters more than polish.

## Bring the right documents

Documents can answer questions that are hard to recall in the moment. Consider bringing:

- A current medication list from your pharmacy, if available.
- Recent lab or imaging reports, if you have them.
- A copy of advance care planning documents, if they exist.
- Insurance or coverage information relevant to the visit.
- A list of questions you want to ask.

If you do not have a document, note that. The clinician may be able to request records or suggest next steps.

## Prepare a medication review

Medication reviews are a common part of geriatric care. To prepare:

1. Gather all containers or a pharmacy printout.
2. Write down what each medication is for, if you know.
3. Note any side effects you have noticed.
4. Note any medications you have stopped and why.
5. Bring questions about timing, interactions, or whether a medication is still needed.

This is not about making changes on your own. It is about giving the clinician accurate information so they can advise you.

## Think about function and daily life

Geriatric care often looks at how someone is functioning day to day. Before the visit, consider:

- **Mobility:** Walking, stairs, getting in and out of chairs, use of a cane or walker.
- **Self-care:** Bathing, dressing, eating, managing medications.
- **Home tasks:** Cooking, cleaning, shopping, managing finances.
- **Memory and thinking:** Any changes noticed by you or others.
- **Mood:** Changes in interest, energy, or outlook.
- **Safety:** Falls, near-falls, driving, or home hazards.

You do not need to have answers for everything. Noticing patterns is enough to start a useful conversation.

## Prepare for the conversation

A first consultation may cover a lot of ground. To make the most of it:

- **Bring a companion if helpful.** A family member or friend can take notes and help recall details.
- **Ask about the plan.** What are the next steps, and who is responsible for each?
- **Ask about uncertainty.** It is reasonable to ask what is known, what is not known, and what might change.
- **Ask about follow-up.** When should you return, and what should prompt an earlier call?
- **Take notes.** Write down key points, or ask your companion to do so.

You can also ask how the geriatrician prefers to communicate between visits, and whether there is a patient portal or care coordinator.

## Questions you might ask

Consider choosing a few questions that matter most to you. Examples:

- What are the most important things to focus on right now?
- Are there medications we should review or simplify?
- What changes should we watch for, and what would warrant a call?
- How will you coordinate with my other clinicians?
- What support services might be helpful, and how would we access them?
- What is the plan for follow-up, and who should I contact with questions?

These questions are general and are not a substitute for individualized advice.

## After the visit

Follow-up is part of preparation, too. After the consultation:

- Review your notes while they are fresh.
- Confirm any referrals, tests, or appointments.
- Update your medication list if changes were made.
- Share relevant information with other clinicians, if appropriate.
- Note any new questions for the next visit.

If something is unclear, it is reasonable to contact the office and ask for clarification.

## Practical checklist

Use this checklist before the appointment:

- [ ] Write down two or three goals for the visit.
- [ ] Create a one-page health summary.
- [ ] Gather a current medication list.
- [ ] Collect relevant reports or documents.
- [ ] Note changes in function, mood, or memory.
- [ ] Write down your top questions.
- [ ] Arrange transportation and a companion if helpful.
- [ ] Bring a notebook or use a notes app.
- [ ] Confirm the appointment time, location, and any preparation instructions.

After the appointment:

- [ ] Review notes and confirm next steps.
- [ ] Schedule follow-up or referrals.
- [ ] Update your records.
- [ ] Share information with other providers as needed.

## A note on expectations

A first consultation is a starting point. It is common for a geriatrician to gather information, ask questions, and outline a plan rather than provide immediate answers. That is normal. The goal is to build a shared understanding and a clear next step.

If you feel rushed or unclear, it is reasonable to say so. You can ask for a summary, a written plan, or a follow-up conversation. Good preparation helps, but it is also okay to ask for help navigating the process.

## When to seek urgent help

This article is not a substitute for professional care. If you or someone you care for has urgent symptoms, contact a qualified healthcare professional or emergency services right away. For non-urgent concerns, speak with a qualified clinician about your specific situation.

## Summary

Preparing for a first geriatrician consultation comes down to a few practical steps: set goals, summarize health information, bring the right documents, prepare for a medication review, think about daily function, and plan for follow-up. A checklist can keep the process manageable. The more clearly you can share what matters to you, the more useful the visit is likely to be.

This article is for general information only and is not medical, legal, or financial advice. Always consult qualified professionals for guidance specific to your situation.

## Start With the Question You Are Actually Trying to Answer

Most people searching for a geriatrician are not trying to rank practices from best to worst. They are trying to answer a smaller, more personal question: *which of these practices is set up to handle the kind of help my family needs right now?*

Service scope is the practical answer to that question. It describes what a practice actually does day to day, how it organizes visits, and where its responsibilities end. Two practices can both be described as "geriatric care" and still differ a great deal in what they offer.

This guide walks through how to compare service scope in a structured way. It offers a checklist you can reuse across practices and a few clearly labeled hypothetical examples. It does not recommend specific providers, does not describe clinical outcomes, and does not replace advice from a qualified professional who knows your situation.

## What "Service Scope" Actually Covers

When you compare practices, it helps to break scope into a few visible dimensions. You can usually get answers to these by reading a practice website, calling the front desk, or asking at a first consultation.

- **Visit format.** In-person, telehealth, home visits, or a mix.
- **Typical visit length and cadence.** How long a first visit tends to run and how often follow-ups are usually scheduled.
- **Who you see.** Whether you consistently see the same clinician or a rotating team.
- **Care coordination.** Whether the practice communicates with other clinicians you already see, and how.
- **On-site services.** Whether things like memory assessment, medication review, or functional assessment are handled in-house or referred out.
- **Communication channels.** Phone, patient portal, or other methods, and typical response expectations.
- **Access and logistics.** Parking, accessibility, transportation considerations, and scheduling lead times.
- **Documentation.** Whether you receive a written summary after visits and what it typically includes.

None of these are quality judgments on their own. They are simply features you can compare side by side.

## A Comparison Checklist You Can Reuse

Use the same checklist for every practice so you are comparing like with like. Write down answers rather than trying to remember them later.

**1. Visit format and location**
- Does the practice offer the format you need (in-person, telehealth, home)?
- If home visits are offered, are they routine or reserved for specific situations?
- How far in advance are appointments typically booked?

**2. The first visit**
- What is the typical length of a first appointment?
- What information is requested beforehand?
- Is a caregiver or family member welcome to attend?

**3. Ongoing care**
- How often are follow-ups usually scheduled?
- Will you see the same clinician each time?
- How are urgent questions handled between visits?

**4. Coordination with others**
- Does the practice routinely communicate with your other clinicians?
- How is that communication documented?
- Who is the point of contact for coordination questions?

**5. Services handled in-house versus referred**
- Which assessments happen at the practice?
- Which are typically referred elsewhere?
- If referred, does the practice help with the referral process?

**6. Communication and records**
- What channels are available for non-urgent questions?
- Do you receive a written summary after visits?
- How do you request records if you change practices?

**7. Practical logistics**
- Is the location accessible for the person attending?
- Are there transportation considerations to plan around?
- What is the process if you need to reschedule?

## Clearly Labeled Examples

The following are hypothetical examples, not real cases, not recommendations, and not predictions of what will happen for you. They illustrate how the checklist can surface differences between practices.

**Example A — A practice oriented around routine in-person visits.**

*This is a hypothetical example.* Suppose a practice offers standard in-person appointments, schedules follow-ups at regular intervals, and refers most specialized assessments to outside clinicians. If your main need is a consistent in-person relationship and you are comfortable coordinating referrals yourself, this scope may line up well. If you need home visits or extensive in-house assessment, the same practice might not fit.

**Example B — A practice oriented around coordination.**

*This is a hypothetical example.* Suppose a different practice emphasizes communication with your existing clinicians and provides written summaries after visits. If you already have several clinicians involved and want one place that helps keep them informed, this scope may be a better match. If you prefer fewer touchpoints and simpler logistics, the added coordination may be more than you need.

**Example C — A practice oriented around flexibility.**

*This is a hypothetical example.* Suppose a third practice offers a mix of telehealth and in-person visits and is explicit about which situations it handles remotely. If travel is difficult, this flexibility might matter a great deal. If the person attending prefers in-person visits and finds remote appointments harder to follow, the same flexibility may be less useful.

In each example, the deciding factor is not which practice is "better." It is which scope matches the specific constraints and preferences in front of you.

## How to Weight What You Find

Once you have answers, the next step is deciding what matters most. A simple approach is to sort your checklist into three buckets.

- **Must-have.** Without this, the practice is not workable (for example, a visit format you can actually get to).
- **Strongly preferred.** Important but potentially negotiable with planning.
- **Nice to have.** Helpful if present, but not decisive.

This keeps you from being swayed by a feature that sounds impressive but does not affect your day-to-day experience. It also makes conversations easier, because you can ask directly about the items in your must-have bucket.

## Questions That Reveal Scope Quickly

If you only have a short call or a brief window at a first meeting, these questions tend to surface scope efficiently:

- "What does a typical first visit look like, and how long is it?"
- "How often do patients usually come back, and do they see the same clinician?"
- "Which parts of care happen here, and which are usually referred out?"
- "How do you communicate with the other clinicians involved?"
- "Do we receive a written summary after visits?"
- "What is the best way to reach you with a non-urgent question?"

Listen for concrete answers. Vague answers are not necessarily a problem, but they are a signal to ask a follow-up.

## Common Pitfalls When Comparing Scope

- **Comparing on a single dimension.** A practice with convenient hours may differ in coordination, and vice versa. Compare across the whole checklist.
- **Assuming broader scope is always better.** More services can mean more coordination on your part. Match scope to need.
- **Relying on memory.** Write answers down as you gather them.
- **Skipping logistics.** Accessibility, travel, and scheduling often decide whether a plan is realistic.
- **Treating a website description as complete.** A short call usually clarifies things a page cannot.

## When to Bring in a Professional

Comparing service scope is a practical exercise, but decisions about care are personal and sometimes complex. If you are unsure how to weigh what you have found, or if the situation involves medical, legal, or financial questions, consult a qualified professional. A clinician, social worker, or advisor who knows your circumstances can help you interpret what you have gathered. This article is general information, not individualized advice.

## Tying It Together

Comparing geriatrician service scope is less about finding a winner and more about finding a fit. Use one checklist, gather concrete answers, label your must-haves clearly, and treat hypothetical scenarios as illustrations rather than predictions. That approach will not tell you what to do, but it will give you a clear, comparable picture so you can make a decision with fewer surprises.

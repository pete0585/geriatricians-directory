# Geriatrician: How to Organize Follow-Up Questions

A first visit with a geriatrician often produces more information than a family can absorb in one sitting. You leave with notes, a list of referrals, possibly new prescriptions, and a vague sense that you meant to ask something important but never got to it.

The follow-up visit is where that loose material gets sorted out. This guide is about how to organize the questions you bring to that next conversation so the time is spent on decisions rather than on re-establishing context.

This is a general planning guide, not medical advice. It does not describe diagnoses, treatments, or outcomes. For anything clinical, rely on the clinician who knows the person's situation.

## Why follow-up questions need their own structure

A first appointment is largely about gathering history. A follow-up is about narrowing. The clinician already has the background; what they need from you is a clear picture of what has changed, what remains unclear, and what decision you are trying to make next.

That shift changes how you should prepare. Instead of a long list of everything you have ever wondered about, you want a short set of questions grouped by purpose. Families who prepare this way tend to spend less time on logistics and more time on the parts of care that actually require a conversation.

## Start by sorting questions into four buckets

Before writing a single question, sort what is on your mind into four rough categories. This step alone makes the list shorter.

1. **Understanding** — questions about what was said or recommended last time.
2. **Coordination** — questions about who is doing what, and how information moves between people.
3. **Practicalities** — scheduling, transportation, paperwork, accessibility, and similar logistics.
4. **Decisions** — questions where you need a recommendation or a choice to be made.

Questions that do not fit any bucket are usually either not yet ripe or belong in a different conversation entirely. Set them aside for now.

## A checklist for building the follow-up list

Use this as a working checklist in the days before the appointment.

- [ ] Review the notes from the last visit and highlight anything you did not fully understand.
- [ ] Write down what has changed since then, in plain language, without trying to interpret it.
- [ ] Note any new medications, referrals, or instructions you received from other people.
- [ ] List the people involved in care and what each of them is responsible for.
- [ ] Identify the one or two decisions you most need to leave the visit with.
- [ ] Turn each of those decisions into a single, specific question.
- [ ] Group the remaining questions under the four buckets above.
- [ ] Cut anything that is purely curiosity unless there is room at the end.
- [ ] Put the most important question first, in case the visit runs short.
- [ ] Decide who will ask the questions and who will take notes.
- [ ] Bring a way to record answers that you can actually read later.
- [ ] Leave space at the bottom for new questions that come up during the visit.

## How to phrase a question so it gets a useful answer

Broad questions produce broad answers. Compare these two:

- "What should we do about Mom's medications?"
- "At the last visit, three medications were reviewed. Can we go through each one and confirm what it is for and whether it should continue?"

The second version tells the clinician what you already know, what you are asking for, and where the conversation should end. It is also easier to answer in the time available.

A useful pattern is: **what we understood last time + what has changed + the specific thing we need decided.** You do not need to use that formula rigidly, but it keeps questions from drifting.

## Hypothetical example: a coordination question

*This is an illustrative example, not a real case.*

Suppose a family is working with a geriatrician, a primary care clinician, and a physical therapist, and information seems to be moving slowly between them. A weak question would be, "Why is communication so bad?" A more workable version:

"We see three people involved in care right now. Can we agree on who is responsible for sharing updates with the others, and what the usual way of doing that is?"

That question is answerable, it does not assign blame, and it produces a concrete answer about process.

## Hypothetical example: an understanding question

*This is an illustrative example, not a real case.*

Suppose a recommendation from the last visit was described in terms the family did not follow. Rather than pretending to understand, a follow-up question could be:

"Last time, a change was suggested and we wrote it down but were not sure we captured it correctly. Could you walk us through it again so we can write it in our own words and check it with you?"

This is not a request for a second opinion or a challenge. It is a request for confirmation, which is a normal and reasonable thing to ask for.

## Hypothetical example: a decision question

*This is an illustrative example, not a real case.*

Suppose the family is trying to decide how often follow-up visits should happen. A vague question — "How often should we come back?" — may get a vague answer. A more useful framing:

"We are trying to plan the next few months. Based on what you know so far, what would you suggest as a reasonable interval before the next visit, and what would make you want to see us sooner?"

The second half of that question is often the more valuable part, because it clarifies what the clinician would consider a meaningful change.

## Questions that are better asked elsewhere

Not every question belongs in a geriatrician follow-up. Some are better directed to a primary care office, a pharmacist, an insurance plan, or an attorney. Recognizing this saves appointment time.

- Billing and coverage questions are usually handled by the practice's administrative staff.
- Medication interaction questions may be best routed to a pharmacist.
- Legal questions about advance directives belong with a qualified attorney.
- Transportation and accessibility logistics are often handled before the visit, not during it.

If you are unsure where a question belongs, it is reasonable to ask the practice in advance so the right person is available.

## How to take notes you will actually use

Notes taken in a hurry are often unreadable a week later. A few habits help:

- Write the question first, then the answer underneath it. This keeps the pairing intact.
- Use the person's own words where you can, and mark places where you are paraphrasing.
- Note anything that was deferred to a later visit, so it does not get lost.
- At the end, read your notes back briefly and ask if anything was recorded incorrectly.

Confirming your notes at the end of the visit is a small step that prevents a lot of confusion later.

## Deciding what to do with the answers

After the visit, sort the answers the same way you sorted the questions. Understanding answers go into your notes. Coordination answers become a short list of who is doing what. Practical answers become calendar items. Decision answers become a plan with a next step.

If a decision was left open, write down what would need to happen for it to be made. That becomes the first question for the next visit.

## When to bring in someone else

If the person receiving care has difficulty following the conversation, or if the family is disagreeing about what was said, it can help to bring a second set of ears. Some practices also have care coordinators or social workers who can help with logistics. Ask the practice what is available.

For anything involving legal documents, financial arrangements, or clinical decisions beyond the scope of the visit, consult a qualified professional in that area.

## A short version of the whole process

1. Sort your questions into understanding, coordination, practicalities, and decisions.
2. Turn each decision into one specific question.
3. Put the most important question first.
4. Write the question above the answer during the visit.
5. Confirm your notes before you leave.
6. Turn the answers into a short list of next steps.

Follow-up visits are most useful when they are narrow. A short, well-ordered list of questions almost always produces more usable information than a long, unstructured one.

# Geriatrician: How to Prepare a Useful Question List

Meeting a geriatrician is an opportunity to get a clearer picture of what matters for an older adult's health and daily life. A well-organized question list helps you use that time well, whether you are the older adult, a family member, or a caregiver helping to coordinate care. This guide is about preparation, not diagnosis or treatment. It offers a practical structure, a checklist, and clearly labeled hypothetical examples to help you decide what to ask. For any medical concern, treatment decision, or legal matter, consult a qualified professional.

## Why a Question List Helps

A consultation can cover a lot of ground: medications, mobility, memory, mood, daily routines, support at home, and coordination with other clinicians. Without a list, it is easy to leave with unanswered questions or to forget something you meant to raise. A list keeps the conversation focused on what matters most to you and the person you are supporting.

A good list is not a script. It is a set of prompts that helps you describe your situation and ask about next steps in a way that fits your goals. It also helps you compare providers if you are still in the process of choosing one.

## Start With Goals, Not Just Problems

Before writing questions, take a few minutes to think about what a good outcome would look like. Goals can be broad, such as staying independent at home, or specific, such as understanding a new symptom. Writing goals first makes it easier to sort questions into what is essential and what can wait.

**Hypothetical example (not a real case):** Suppose an older adult wants to keep cooking and gardening but has noticed more fatigue. A goal might be "understand what might be contributing to fatigue and what supports could help maintain daily activities." Questions could then focus on how the geriatrician approaches fatigue, what information would help, and how follow-up would work.

## A Checklist for Building Your List

Use this checklist as a starting point. Adapt it to your situation and the older adult's preferences.

- **Identify the main concerns.** List two or three issues that feel most important right now.
- **Note what has changed.** Write down when a change started and how it affects daily life.
- **List current medications and supplements.** Include doses if you have them, and note who manages refills.
- **Note other clinicians involved.** Primary care, specialists, therapists, or home care staff.
- **Write down questions about the first visit.** What will happen, how long it takes, and who should attend.
- **Prepare questions about communication.** How results and plans will be shared, and with whom.
- **Ask about coordination.** How the geriatrician works with other providers.
- **Ask about follow-up.** How often visits occur and what triggers a check-in.
- **Ask about support resources.** What community or practical supports may be relevant.
- **Leave space for new questions.** The conversation may raise topics you had not considered.

## Questions About the First Visit

These questions help you understand what to expect and how to prepare.

- What should we bring to the first appointment?
- How long does a typical first visit take?
- Should the older adult attend alone, or is it helpful to have a family member or caregiver present?
- Will you review medications and daily routines?
- What information would help you understand our situation better?
- How do you decide what to focus on first?

**Hypothetical example (not a real case):** A caregiver might ask, "If we bring a list of medications and a short description of a typical day, would that be useful for the first visit?" This is a preparation question, not a request for a diagnosis.

## Questions About Communication and Coordination

Clear communication can reduce confusion and help everyone stay on the same page.

- How will you share findings or recommendations with us?
- Will you send information to the primary care clinician or other specialists?
- How can we reach you with a non-urgent question?
- What is the best way to share updates between visits?
- If we see other providers, how do you coordinate care?

## Questions About Follow-Up and Ongoing Support

Understanding what happens after the first visit helps you plan.

- How often would follow-up visits be scheduled?
- What kinds of changes would prompt an earlier check-in?
- Are there supports or resources you commonly suggest for daily living?
- How do you work with family members or caregivers over time?
- What should we do if a new concern comes up between visits?

## Questions If You Are Comparing Providers

If you are still choosing a geriatrician, your list can help you compare approaches. Focus on fit, communication, and practical logistics rather than on claims about outcomes.

- What is your approach to working with older adults and their families?
- How do you involve the older adult in decisions?
- What is your typical process for a first visit and follow-up?
- How do you handle communication with other providers?
- What should we consider when deciding whether to work with you?

## Questions About Practical Logistics

Logistics can shape whether a provider is a good fit. These questions are about process, not pricing details.

- Where are visits held, and is there an option for virtual visits?
- How is scheduling handled, and how far in advance are appointments booked?
- What should we do if we need to reschedule?
- Who should we contact for administrative questions?

If cost is a concern, prepare questions about how fees and billing are explained. You can find a starting point in [Geriatrician: How to Get Clarity About Fees](https://geriatriciandirectory.com/blog/geriatrician-how-to-get-clarity-about-fees).

## Preparing for the Conversation Itself

A few simple habits make a question list easier to use.

- **Prioritize.** Put the most important questions at the top.
- **Keep it short.** One page is often enough.
- **Use plain language.** Write questions the way you would say them.
- **Bring a pen.** Note answers or new questions as they come up.
- **Ask for clarification.** If an answer is unclear, it is reasonable to ask for it to be explained again.
- **Agree on next steps.** Before the visit ends, confirm what happens next and who is responsible.

**Hypothetical example (not a real case):** A family might prepare three priority questions about a recent change in daily functioning, two questions about coordination with a primary care clinician, and one question about follow-up timing. They leave space at the bottom for notes.

## Adapting the List for Different Situations

The same structure can be adjusted for different needs.

- **If the older adult has memory concerns:** Ask how the geriatrician gathers information from both the older adult and those who know them well.
- **If multiple providers are involved:** Ask how care is coordinated and how information is shared.
- **If the older adult lives alone:** Ask about practical supports and how changes are monitored over time.
- **If a caregiver is heavily involved:** Ask how the caregiver's observations are included and how communication is handled.

## What to Avoid in Your List

A useful list stays focused on preparation and understanding. It is best to avoid questions that ask for a diagnosis or treatment promise before an evaluation, or that request information a provider cannot responsibly give without seeing the older adult. If you have an urgent medical concern, seek appropriate care rather than waiting for a scheduled visit.

## Final Thoughts

A question list is a tool for clarity. It helps you describe your situation, understand how a geriatrician works, and plan next steps. Keep it practical, prioritize what matters most, and leave room for the conversation to grow.
